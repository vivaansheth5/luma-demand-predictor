# Methods

This document states precisely what each part of the system computes. The README explains why each part exists; this is the reference for how.

Notation: $s$ is a store, $k$ a product, $z$ a size, $t$ a day. A *pair* is one product in one store. A *cell* is one size of one product in one store.

## 0. Input

Luma reads tables (CSV or Parquet) through `luma.data.loader` and turns them into one object, a `Panel`. Sales and a catalog are required. Everything else is optional, and what is missing changes what the run can do:

| Supplied | Effect |
|---|---|
| `sales` without `stock_open` and `stock_close` | Sales are taken as demand. Section 2 is skipped, so sold-out days read as low demand |
| No `stock` table | Sections 8 and 9 are skipped. The agent stops at forecasts, trends and anomalies |
| No coordinates in `stores`, or one store | Section 9 is skipped |
| No `events` or `calendar` table | The calendar carries weekday, pay cycle and season only. A festival or sale the model has not been told about is not forecast |
| No `buzz` table | Trends are read from sales alone, and no value can be called Emerging |
| No `unit_cost` | Cost is taken as 45% of price |

Catalog columns holding text with 2 to 40 distinct values are treated as product attributes. Each one enters the forecaster as a categorical feature and is tested for trends (section 6). One attribute is the *trend attribute*: the one the buzz table is keyed by (`style` when the catalog has it, otherwise the first attribute).

A history needs at least 110 days. Replaying an earlier forecast date needs 28 more days per date, so a 20-week history is scored on one earlier date, and four dates need about 32 weeks. Luma has been run at 800 product and store pairs (the demo). Its arrays are dense over days, stores, products and sizes, so a very large catalog will need a sparse layout first.

The demo runs the same pipeline on a simulated retailer, described next, because a simulator can supply what real data cannot: true demand.

## 1. The simulated demo

The demo history is generated by `luma.sim`: eight stores in Bangalore, 100 products in four sizes, 80 weeks. Expected demand for a pair on a day is a product of factors:

$$
\lambda_{s,k,t} = \text{base}_k \cdot \text{store}_{s,t} \cdot \text{affinity}_{s,\text{cat}(k)} \cdot \text{lifecycle}_k(t) \cdot \text{season}_{\text{cat}(k)}(t) \cdot \text{events}_{\text{cat}(k)}(t) \cdot \text{trend}_{\text{style}(k)}(t - \delta_s) \cdot \text{price}_{s,k,t} \cdot \text{shock}_{s,k,t}
$$

| Factor | What it models |
|---|---|
| store | Catchment size, a ramp after opening, network growth, weekday pattern, payday, rain, a daily store-wide shock |
| affinity | Each store's taste for each category |
| lifecycle | Launch ramp, plateau, decay with a product-specific half-life |
| season | Annual cycle per category (outerwear peaks in winter) |
| events | Festivals with a pre-festival build and a post-festival dip, sale events, sports seasons, back-to-campus weeks |
| trend | A latent multiplier per style, read with a store-specific delay $\delta_s$ so some stores adopt trends later |
| price | Discount response with a product elasticity scaled by the store's price sensitivity |
| shock | Injected viral product spikes and one-day store outages |

Demand is drawn as $D_{s,k,t} \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda \cdot G)$ with $G \sim \text{Gamma}(\kappa, 1/\kappa)$, which is negative binomial with variance $\lambda + \lambda^2/\kappa$. It is split into sizes by a multinomial with a true size mix per pair.

Sales come from running inventory forward under a status-quo policy: each Sunday, every cell is topped up to 21 days of its recent in-stock selling rate, with a 7-day supplier lead time. Sales are $\min(\text{demand}, \text{stock})$. The gap between demand and sales is what the models must recover and what real data never shows.

A buzz index per style is a noisy reading of where the style's trend multiplier will be ten days later.

The simulator also holds 28 further days of demand that no model sees. They are used once, to score decisions.

## 2. Uncensoring

A cell-day that ended with zero stock is right-censored: demand was at least the units sold. For $D \sim \text{Poisson}(\mu)$ and $s$ units sold,

$$
E[D \mid D \ge s] = \mu \cdot \frac{P(D \ge s-1)}{P(D \ge s)} .
$$

The rate $\mu$ for a cell-day is a local selling rate times the store's traffic index for that day. The local rate is a ratio of sums over a window of 28 days either side, shrunk towards the cell's all-time rate and then towards the pooled rate of its store, category and size. Rates and imputed values are refined together for three rounds.

Two outputs are kept. `demand` fills censored cells with the conditional mean and is the training target for expected demand. `draw` fills them with one draw from the conditional distribution and is used for quantile training and calibration, because means are smoother than outcomes and intervals learned from means come out too narrow.

The window and prior strength were tuned on the simulator so that total recovered demand matches total lost demand.

## 3. Forecast targets and features

One row is one question: at the end of day $o$ (the origin), how many units will this pair sell over the window that starts $a$ days ahead and lasts $n$ days? Ten windows are used, all multiples of 7 days so every window has the same weekday mix: weeks 1 to 4 separately, and cumulative spans of 14, 21 and 28 days from several starts.

The target is scaled by the pair's run-rate:

$$
y = \frac{\sum_{t=o+a}^{o+a+n-1} D_{s,k,t}}{n \cdot \ell_{s,k,o}}, \qquad
\ell_{s,k,o} = \frac{\text{demand in last 28 days} + m \cdot \bar r_{s,\text{cat}}}{\text{days live in last 28} + m}
$$

where $\bar r_{s,\text{cat}}$ is the average rate per product of that category in that store and $m = 7$. A product with three days of history therefore starts near its category's rate.

Features (68 on the demo; one more for each catalog attribute) fall into the families the dashboard reports as drivers: recent level, momentum ratios, pooled momentum of the product across all stores and the category in the store, momentum of the trend attribute and of its buzz, calendar channels over the last 7 and 28 days and over the target window, planned discounts, season, product age, and attributes. Every history feature reads data up to the origin only. `tests/test_models.py::test_features_do_not_read_the_future` checks this by corrupting all later data and asserting the features do not change.

## 4. Models

All models are LightGBM and share one feature matrix.

* Expected demand: Tweedie loss with variance power 1.2, which handles many zeros and a log link.
* Quantiles 0.10, 0.25, 0.50, 0.75, 0.90, 0.95: pinball loss, trained on the `draw` target.

Rows are weighted by $\ell^{0.75}\sqrt{n/7}$ so the loss follows unit volume without ignoring slow sellers. Overlapping windows make rows redundant, so a random 40% is used. In testing this cut training time by more than half and moved the error by less than 0.3 points.

Predictions are multiplied back by $n \cdot \ell$. Quantiles are sorted to remove crossing, and cumulative windows are made non-decreasing in length.

### Calibration

The pipeline replays four earlier forecast dates, 28 days apart, each trained only on data up to its own date. When a replayed forecast's horizon has passed, its quantile errors are recorded against demand as estimated at that later date (the `draw`, never simulator truth):

$$
e_{i,\tau} = \frac{y_i - \hat q_{i,\tau}}{\sqrt{\hat\mu_i + 1}} .
$$

Each forecast date's median error at the median quantile is subtracted from all its errors, because a level miss belongs to one period and does not transfer. The shift for quantile $\tau$ and window length $n$ is the $\tau$-quantile of the pooled, centred errors, and the calibrated quantile is $\hat q_\tau + \text{shift}_{\tau,n}\sqrt{\hat\mu + 1}$. This is split conformal calibration applied per quantile, with errors collected the way a weekly production job would collect them.

### From product to size

Stock is held by size. The product-level forecast becomes a negative binomial with mean $\hat\mu$ and shape chosen so that its spread matches the calibrated P10 to P90 width. A negative binomial thinned by a size share $p$ is again negative binomial with mean $p\hat\mu$ and the same shape, which gives every cell a full distribution without a second model. `tests/test_models.py::test_thinning_keeps_the_shape` verifies the property numerically.

### Explanations

For the Tweedie model, LightGBM's exact TreeSHAP values are additive in log space. Summing them within a feature family and exponentiating gives a factor per family, shown in the dashboard as a percentage effect on the forecast.

## 5. Size curves

Counts are taken only from days on which no size of the pair ended sold out, which removes the bias that a sold-out size looks unpopular. The curve for a pair is the posterior mean of a Dirichlet-multinomial:

$$
\hat p_{s,k} = \frac{n_{s,k} + \kappa \, \pi_{s,k}}{N_{s,k} + \kappa}, \qquad
\pi_{s,k} \propto \frac{p^{\text{product}}_k \cdot p^{\text{store,cat}}_{s,\text{cat}(k)}}{p^{\text{cat}}_{\text{cat}(k)}} .
$$

The prior combines the product's curve across all stores with the store's curve for the category. Each concentration $\kappa$ is fitted by maximising the Dirichlet-multinomial likelihood.

## 6. Trend detection

For every value of every catalog attribute (style, colour, fit, fabric, collection, whatever the catalog holds), the weekly share of demand is computed inside each category and recombined with fixed category weights, which removes category seasonality. On the last 8 weekly values the detector computes the Mann-Kendall statistic and p-value, the Theil-Sen slope, and Benjamini-Hochberg q-values across all attributes tested. Status:

* Rising or Fading: $q \le 0.10$, by the sign of the slope. Rising becomes Peaking if buzz momentum is below -5%.
* Emerging: not yet significant, buzz up more than 10% over 28 days, share change positive. This needs a buzz table.
* Stable: otherwise.

## 7. Anomaly detection

Three levels are monitored, each relative to its parent: the total across stores, each store relative to the total, each product relative to its category. For a series, the expected value of a day is the median of the previous 28 days times a weekday factor from the same window. Surprise is $2\sqrt{y + 3/8} - 2\sqrt{\hat y + 3/8}$ (Anscombe), divided by the series' trailing robust spread, flagged above 4.0. Products are tested on three-day totals. A store or product flag also needs a ratio of at least 1.6 either way.

Each flag is checked against known causes: the event calendar and heavy rain for the total, discount changes and launch weeks for products. Store flags have no known cause by construction, because causes that touch every store are divided out.

## 8. Stock risk and supplier orders

For each cell, with stock position $x$ (on hand plus inbound) and demand $D$ over the 14-day protection interval (7 days of lead time plus 7 days to the next order):

* stockout risk is $P(D > x)$;
* the order-up-to level is the service-level quantile of $D$, with service levels 95%, 90% and 80% for classes A, B and C by share of the store's forecast revenue;
* expected unmet demand is $E[(D - x)^+]$.

When the budget cannot fund every order, the $n$-th extra unit of a cell has expected value $\text{margin} \cdot P(D \ge x + n)$. Units are bought in descending order of that value per unit of cost until the budget is spent. Because each cell has diminishing returns and cells are independent, this greedy order is optimal up to the last unit bought.

## 9. Transfers

Transfers cover the 7 days before a supplier order can land. Let $a_{i,u}$ be the probability that donor store $i$ would have sold its $u$-th spare unit of a cell in that time, and $b_{j,v}$ the probability that receiver $j$ sells its $v$-th extra unit. With unit variables $g, r \in [0,1]$, integer flows $x$, and binary lane variables $y$:

$$
\max \sum_{c} m_c \Big( \sum_{j,v} b_{c,j,v}\, r_{c,j,v} - \sum_{i,u} a_{c,i,u}\, g_{c,i,u} \Big) - \sum_{c,i,j} h_{ij}\, x_{c,i,j} - \sum_{i,j} F_{ij}\, y_{ij}
$$

subject to

$$
\sum_j x_{c,i,j} = \sum_u g_{c,i,u}, \qquad \sum_i x_{c,i,j} = \sum_v r_{c,j,v}, \qquad \sum_c x_{c,i,j} \le \text{capacity} \cdot y_{ij} .
$$

$m_c$ is unit margin, $h_{ij}$ a per-unit handling cost that grows with distance, $F_{ij}$ the fixed cost of a trip. Because $a$ rises and $b$ falls with each unit, the solver takes units in the right order and the objective stays linear. The problem is solved with HiGHS through `scipy.optimize.milp` (about 8,000 variables on the full network, solved to optimality in a few seconds).

## 10. Closed-loop evaluation

Each policy starts from the same stock position on the as-of evening and runs through the same 28 hidden days. Orders are placed every Sunday evening and arrive 7 days later; transfers arrive the next morning.

The agent inside the loop re-runs its own pipeline every Sunday on what it could observe: the sales and stock its own decisions produced, the buzz index to date, the planned calendar. The model trained at the as-of date is reused without retraining.

Both policies are swept across their one dial (days of cover; service level) to trace service against average stock value. The headline agent run uses the default service levels, transfers, and the weekly supplier budget.

Reported measures: fill rate (units sold over units demanded), lost revenue, average stock value at cost, transfer cost, and net contribution (gross margin minus transfer cost minus holding cost at 24% a year).

## 11. Limits

* The headline results come from one simulated world. The simulator's structure resembles what the features can express. Real demand will be less tidy, and the gains will be smaller.
* On a brand's own data there is no true demand to score against. Forecasts are scored against what was sold next, and no closed-loop test is run, so the value of the stock decisions is not measured.
* With a few dozen products the model has little to learn from, and one fast-growing product can dominate a forecast. The brief names such a product when it accounts for most of a large change.
* The status quo is a single reasonable rule, not a tuned competitor.
* Supplier lead time is fixed at 7 days with no minimum order quantities, and returns are not modelled.
* Calibration uses four earlier forecast dates. Level misses tied to one period (a festival the model under-read) are not corrected.
* The 80% interval covers about 76% of outcomes: low quantiles run high on slow sellers. Upper quantiles, which drive stock decisions, are on target.
* The buzz index is simulated. A real one (search interest, social mentions) will be noisier and its lead time will vary.
